## Brief Summary

**Audience**: Engineering managers and startup CTOs
**One takeaway**: This person ships hard mobile problems that others cannot or will not
**Proof anchor**: 24/7 background audio recording on iOS — battery, memory, background kills, Swift + ObjC bridging, 3-4% battery per hour, three rewrites
**Tone**: Calm, direct, unembellished. Present tense. No hedging. No warm greeting energy.
**Constraints**: Headline includes inline photo. Under 30 words total across headline + sub-line.

---

## The Five Options

---

### Option 1

**Headline:**
`[Photo] Jatin. I solve the iOS problems that don't have Stack Overflow answers.`

**Sub-line:**
`24/7 background audio on iOS. Three rewrites. 3-4% battery per hour. It shipped.`

**Word count:** 27

**Why it works:**

"Don't have Stack Overflow answers" is instantly legible to any engineer and names the domain precisely — not "hard problems" in the abstract, but the specific class of problems that live in undocumented platform behavior. A CTO who has been burned by this before will recognize it immediately.

"It shipped" is the hammer. Two words at the end of the sub-line do more work than any adjective could. It is the unembellished form of confidence: no claiming, just stating.

**Risk:** "Stack Overflow" is a cultural reference that may date faster than other options. Also assumes the audience knows what the absence of documentation means operationally — most CTOs will, but the reference is slightly insider.

---

### Option 2

**Headline:**
`[Photo] Jatin — Mobile Lead. Hard iOS constraints are where I work best.`

**Sub-line:**
`24/7 background audio that survives battery kills, memory pressure, and background suspension. Swift + ObjC bridging. Shipped.`

**Word count:** 29

**Why it works:**

"Hard iOS constraints are where I work best" is a direct, verifiable claim — not "I'm great at hard problems" but a specific environment and a specific relationship to that environment. It signals comfort where others feel friction. That is the differentiator.

The sub-line is a technical inventory without explanation. A hiring manager who understands iOS will parse "battery kills, memory pressure, background suspension" and know exactly how hard this is. One who does not will still understand "Swift + ObjC bridging" as a signal of depth.

**Risk:** "Where I work best" is the weakest phrase in this option. It sounds slightly like a job interview answer. Could tighten to "Hard iOS constraints are my edge."

---

### Option 3

**Headline:**
`[Photo] Jatin. I ship the mobile problems others scope out.`

**Sub-line:**
`Latest: 24/7 audio recording on iOS — surviving background kills, memory pressure, and Apple's undocumented edge cases.`

**Word count:** 28

**Why it works:**

"Scope out" names the failure mode from the CTO's perspective, not Jatin's. Every engineering manager has been in a sprint planning where someone said "that's out of scope" — meaning too hard, too risky, or someone does not know how. This phrase puts the reader in their own experience before making the claim.

"Latest" implies a pattern rather than a single achievement. It signals that this is the most recent difficult thing, not the only one.

**Risk:** "Scope out" may read as casual to some readers. It is colloquial, which fits the brand voice but is slightly at odds with the "calm, unembellished confidence" register. Also, "Latest:" as a label adds a slightly informal structure.

---

### Option 4 — Recommended

**Headline:**
`[Photo] Jatin. Mobile Lead. I build what Apple doesn't document.`

**Sub-line:**
`24/7 background audio on iOS — battery, memory, background suspension. All three solved. Three rewrites.`

**Word count:** 24

**Why it works:**

"I build what Apple doesn't document" is the highest-information phrase across all five options. It says:
- Platform: iOS
- Difficulty: Beyond official guidance
- Outcome: It exists and works

It cannot be written by someone who has not lived in that space. A mid-career engineer who has only worked in documented API surface area cannot claim this authentically — and a CTO evaluating senior hires will sense that.

The sub-line enumerates the constraints — battery, memory, background suspension — then closes with "All three solved." That structure builds tension and releases it in three words. "Three rewrites" adds the honest, unembellished detail that makes the claim credible rather than boastful. It shows that the work was hard in practice, not just in theory.

The total word count of 24 reads as confidence. There is no explaining, no hedging, no softening.

**Risk:** Lowest risk of the five options. "I build what Apple doesn't document" is slightly abstract on its own, which is why the sub-line must do the grounding work. If the sub-line were removed, the headline would need more specificity.

---

### Option 5

**Headline:**
`[Photo] Jatin — the engineer you call when mobile goes past the docs.`

**Sub-line:**
`24/7 iOS audio recording: background kills, memory pressure, Swift/ObjC bridging, 3-4% battery per hour. Shipped to production.`

**Word count:** 29

**Why it works:**

"The engineer you call" is a category claim, not a self-description. It positions Jatin not as a candidate but as a resource — the kind of person who gets a specific type of call. This is a different psychological register from Options 1-4, which all use first-person identity statements.

"When mobile goes past the docs" mirrors the language of "I build what Apple doesn't document" in Option 4 but reframes it as a situation the reader has faced, not a claim Jatin makes about himself. It invites recognition rather than asking for belief.

The sub-line is the most technically specific of all five options — it lists the constraints by name and closes with "Shipped to production," which is the most credible possible ending for a technical proof point.

**Risk:** "The engineer you call" is the highest-confidence register of the five. It requires Jatin to be fully comfortable with that framing. If it feels performed rather than earned, it undermines the calm tone the positioning requires. It is also a slight departure from the first-person constraint implicit in `I'm [Photo] Jatin` — though the headline can be restructured as `[Photo] Jatin — the engineer you call...` without losing the photo integration.

---

## Recommendation

**Option 4** is the strongest execution of the locked positioning.

The reasons are specific:

1. "I build what Apple doesn't document" is the single best phrase across all five options. It is precise, non-generic, and self-qualifying — only someone who has operated in undocumented iOS platform behavior can write it authentically.

2. The sub-line structure — enumerate constraints, state resolution, add honest detail — is the most disciplined execution of "calm, unembellished confidence" in the set.

3. Word count of 24 is the shortest. In this register, shorter reads as more confident.

4. No warm greeting energy. No hedging. No philosophy. No list of technologies. Every word earns its place.

**Option 3 is the strongest alternative.** If Option 4 feels too terse in browser review, Option 3 provides more warmth through the "scope out" framing without sacrificing directness.

**Option 5 is highest upside, highest risk.** If the third-person category claim ("the engineer you call") fits Jatin's personality authentically, it is a more memorable opening than any first-person option. If it reads as performed, it undermines the tone the entire positioning is built on. This is a judgment call only Jatin can make.

---

## On the "Hey" Question

None of these options use "Hey" or any greeting construction. This is intentional.

The brief says "calm, direct, unembellished confidence" and "direct declaration." A greeting establishes a social register before a competence signal. For an audience of CTOs evaluating a senior mobile hire, the social register is not what creates the decision to keep reading — the competence signal is.

The inline profile photo carries the human warmth that "Hey" was previously doing. The photo creates the human moment. The copy creates the competence signal. These are separate jobs and they are now performed by separate elements.

## Implementation Note

All five options maintain the `[Photo] Jatin` structure. The photo token sits between the opening and the name in Options 1, 3, and 4 (where the sentence opens without "Hey" or a greeting). In Options 2 and 5, the dash construction accommodates the inline photo naturally.

The sub-line in all five options should render at a smaller size with `text-secondary` color treatment, consistent with the current implementation.
